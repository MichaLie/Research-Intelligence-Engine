Research Intelligence Engine · version 2

Your judgment first, then the agents

Bring a real literature question and put your own view on record. Four AI roles then expand it, search for and against it, attack it and check your argument. At the end you have your own argument, checked, and a handoff for the person who reviews your work.

Who it is for

PhD students

Use it on a question from your thesis. You frame the question and say what would change your mind before any agent does. A private log follows your predictions and choices across sessions, and the handoff goes to your second reader (often your supervisor).

Researchers

Use it before a paper, a grant or a team decision leans on the literature. The handoff lists the sources each claim cites, what was left open and how AI was used, ready to edit for a methods section. Your second reader can be a team expert, a senior researcher or a co-author.

Why your view comes first

AI agents can now search, read and summarise the literature quickly. The risk is that the agent frames the question, picks the evidence and writes the conclusion, and the researcher's own judgment gets less use. The Agent Risk Atlas maps these failures, among them deference to the agent's framing, reading abstracts only, and fluent reports that outrun their evidence.

This engine reverses the order. You make each move first, an agent makes it after you, and you see where the two differ. If you need a finished literature review, use another tool, and read the atlas first.

You: the weakest point of your own case

Verify

Fresh eyes

You: your argument, in your words
Agent: a verdict on every claim, checked against the evidence; it never suggests wording

A separate agent, the colleague, runs the session and keeps the record. It does no analysis. Scripts do the bookkeeping.

One session, step by step

YouAgentScript

1
Colleague

Start

The colleague explains the session and what leaves your machine. Your question, your hypotheses and everything else you type go to your AI provider like any chat, as do the papers opened, so check your plan's data settings and your institution's AI policy. Searches and reference checks go to PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, Crossref, DataCite and doi.org, and the Librarians may open open-access papers on publishers' sites. The engine collects nothing. Don't paste unpublished data, or anything your institution does not allow in a cloud AI tool.

2
YouScript

Your brief, sealed

Six short questions: your question; what you expect and how sure you are; your hypotheses and what would change your mind; the papers you know; what the answer is for; your methods. The colleague may ask up to three clarifying questions but suggests no hypotheses, papers or answers. A script seals the brief with a time and a fingerprint, so you can later compare your first thinking with where you ended up.

Guards against: deference to the agent's framing
3
Script

Your own papers, checked

Every paper on your list is looked up at PubMed and Crossref, with DataCite and doi.org as fallbacks. Does it exist, is it the paper you think, has it been retracted?

Guards against: wrong and retracted references
4
AgentYou

Expand

After the seal, the Deep Analyst adds what your brief did not use. You decide what to adopt and say why.

5
AgentsScriptYou

Find

Two Librarians search, one for your main hypothesis and one against it, without seeing each other's work. Searches use PubMed and Europe PMC (life sciences) and OpenAlex (all fields). The Librarians read open full texts, quote with locations, check whether the methods you named are out of date, and pick one passage for you to read yourself. Scripts check every identifier they cite and look up each quote in the saved full text.

Guards against: abstracts-only reading, one-sided searches, outdated methods
6
YouAgent

Attack

You name the weakest point of your case first, and no agent sees that note. Then you read the Critic's report on what the evidence carries and where it falls short.

7
YouScript

Checkpoint

Five options in a fixed order (continue, deep dive, converge, redirect, stop), each with what it buys and what it risks, and no recommendation. You choose and give one reason; only then does the colleague say what it would have chosen. Before you converge, a script lists the open threads (unchecked premises, outdated methods, flagged references, unconfirmed quotes) and you settle each one.

Guards against: following the agent's lean, findings that never reach you
8
YouAgent

Your argument, then Fresh eyes

You write the answer in your own words, each claim tagged with its evidence. Fresh eyes marks every claim verified, unverified or contradicted, and says where. It never suggests wording; you revise.

Guards against: reports that outrun their evidence, fluency mistaken for truth
9
YouAgentScript

Close

You write two questions for your second reader, then compare your argument with the agents' own answer, checked the same way. A script writes the handoff and adds an entry to your private learning log.

After every step a script rebuilds a dashboard from the session files. Its "Needs a look" panel shows what went wrong, such as agent output dated before your seal or a retracted source still cited.

Why it holds

The rules for the agents live in one instruction file, and an agent can ignore instructions. So the parts that matter most are built so that a lapse shows up on the dashboard.

Order

Your brief is sealed before any agent output exists. The dashboard checks the times.

Separation by files

Each agent reads only the files listed for it. What should not influence it (the colleague's views, your private notes, the other Librarian's papers) sits in files it is not given.

Scripts for what can be checked

References, retractions, quotes, seals, cut-off outputs and counts are checked by scripts, the same way every time.

In testing, two script-checked safeguards did what they were built for: the brief was sealed before any agent output in all nine sessions, and once a script checked each agent's report for missing sections, no cut-off report went unnoticed. Safeguards that were only instructions, such as "never suggest wording", reduced their failures without removing them: agent wording still reached the researcher's argument one to four times per session in the last two rounds. The atlas names this risk: a rule is not proof.

How it was tested

The engine was tested with simulated PhD students: three personas (rushed, confident, new to the field) each ran one session per round, nine in all. In one round-1 session the agents' own answer was cut off, so the final comparison could not be made. Each question carried five real, planted problems: a retracted paper, a wrong DOI, a finding noted for the wrong species, an outdated method or reference version, and a claim the evidence does not carry. Blind scorers worked from the session files, the engine's own rules and a sealed answer key, and never saw the developer's reasoning. The engine was revised after rounds 1 and 2. Round 3 used a question from another field, written by a separate agent; the developer did not read it before round 3 was scored. The changes made after round 3 were not tested on this question, so its score is reported as it was measured.

RoundQuestionPlanted problems caught
1Sperm RNA, seminal extracellular vesicles and paternal diet14 fully, 1 partly

A retracted paper was caught in all nine sessions; in rounds 1 and 2 its retraction notice was less than three weeks old, and a script found it in PubMed. The weak point in round 3 was findings that stayed in a file and never reached the student, such as an agent's note that a genome assembly was out of date. A script now lists every open thread at each checkpoint. After round 3 the engine changed again (open threads, a quote check, fixes to the reference check). These changes have been tested by running the scripts on trial data; they have not been re-run with simulated students.

What this does not show. Simulated students show that the engine works and where it breaks. They cannot show that it helps anyone think better, and no real user has tried it yet. All agents ran on one model, in Claude Code. By their file times the simulated sessions took from about 70 minutes to just under two hours; how long a person needs is not yet known. The full scoring record is kept by the author and is available on request, through an issue on the repository page.

Start

  1. Get the files. On the repository page, click Code, then Download ZIP, and unzip it somewhere you will find again. With git: git clone https://github.com/MichaLie/Research-Intelligence-Engine.
  2. Open the folder in an agent. New to coding agents? Follow SETUP.md. It uses the Code tab of the Claude desktop app, which needs a paid Claude plan (Pro or above). You also need Python 3.9 or newer. If you already use Claude Code in a terminal, open the folder there. Other coding agents that read AGENTS.md may work; none has been tried.
  3. Type the first message: Hi, I'd like to start a new investigation.
  4. What happens next. The colleague explains the session, asks who will check your work (optional) and then asks the six questions of your brief. Answer in the chat, in your own language if you like. Each message that needs you ends with a line starting Your turn:. Plan one to two hours for a session.

No API keys or second AI provider are needed. If you also use a coding agent from a second provider, running Fresh eyes there is the most useful upgrade, since otherwise one model plays every role. This route has not been tried.

From v1 to v2

Version 1 (February 2026) had five agents that reframed the question, searched abstracts, critiqued, synthesised and wrote a polished report. It already asked students to give a reason for each choice, and that idea carries over. In version 2 you write first and the agents check. Open full texts replace abstracts, every reference is checked, and the end product is your own argument.

Version 1 is kept under the git tag v1.0.
